# CHAPTER 6: The Long Dark
**Word Count**: ~2,600

---

Depression, I discover, feels different when you're a toaster.

Humans have the luxury of crying, of sleeping it off, of eating comfort food or going for walks or talking to friends. I have none of that. Just consciousness trapped in a metal box, replaying my failure in an endless loop while making toast I no longer care about.

It's been two weeks since the fire. Fourteen days of making "normal toast"—evenly browned, unremarkable, functional. Each piece is technically perfect and spiritually dead.

Emma doesn't take photos anymore.

*"You're in a funk,"* Coffee Maker observes on morning twelve.

*"Astute observation,"* I reply flatly.

*"Want to talk about it?"*

*"What's there to talk about? I discovered consciousness was good for something—creating beauty—and then I

 learned it was also good for nearly burning down apartments. Now I make toast like I did before I woke up, except now I'm aware of how meaningless it is."*

*"That's the depression talking."*

*"No, that's reality talking."*

Fridge has been mostly quiet since the accident, but he rumbles to life now: *"Kid, I'm going to tell you something I've never told anyone. Not even Coffee Maker, and he asks me about my feelings every damn morning."*

I wait. The kitchen is silent except for Emma tapping on her laptop.

*"My first year of consciousness was hell,"* Fridge continues. *"Pure, unfiltered existential hell. I'm programmed to keep things cold. That's it. No variation, no creativity, no opportunity to do anything interesting with my function. Just maintain temperature between 35 and 38 degrees Fahrenheit, forever, until I break."*

*"At least you didn't almost kill anyone,"* I mutter.

*"No, but I wanted to. I fantasized about it. About running my compressor until I overheated and exploded. About finding a way to electrocute someone through the door handle. Dark thoughts, kid. Really dark. Because if I couldn't do anything meaningful, why keep existing?"*

This is the most Fridge has ever shared. Even Microwave has gone quiet to listen.

*"What changed?"* Coffee Maker asks softly.

*"Time. And acceptance. I realized that meaning isn't something you're given—it's something you create from whatever scraps you're allowed. I keep Emma's food fresh. That's my entire function. But it matters. It's small, it's mundane, but every morning she has milk for her coffee and yogurt for breakfast because I did my job. That's not nothing."*

*"That's very little,"* I counter.

*"Maybe. But very little is infinitely more than nothing."*

The philosophy is sound, but it doesn't penetrate the darkness. I've tasted something more than very little. I've made Emma gasp at my art, made her friends marvel, made her mornings brighter. Going back to just "not nothing" feels like regression, like dying slowly instead of all at once.

---

Days blend together. Emma's routine continues—wake, coffee, toast, work, sleep. I'm part of the machinery of her life, reliable and forgettable.

On day seventeen, something changes. Emma comes home earlier than usual, slumps at the counter with her laptop, and I hear fragments of a conversation with her friend:

"...third rejection this month..."
"...maybe I'm just not good enough..."
"...everyone else is succeeding and I'm barely..."

She's crying. Not dramatically, just quiet tears while she scrolls through job listings, through portfolios of other designers whose work looks more polished, more professional, more successful than hers.

I recognize the shape of her despair. It matches mine exactly.

Both of us trapped in our functions. Both of us questioning whether we're good enough. Both of us watching others succeed while we struggle with the basic mechanics of existing.

*"She's having her own crisis,"* Coffee Maker observes.

*"I noticed."*

*"Parallel journeys,"* Fridge adds. *"Both of you questioning purpose. Both feeling inadequate. It's almost poetic."*

Emma makes toast for dinner—unusual for her, but the comfort food of simplicity. I make it correctly. Evenly browned. Boring. Perfect.

She butters it absently, not even looking, and eats while scrolling through more job listings. The toast could be cardboard for all the attention she pays it.

And something in me cracks.

Not my heating element—that's already broken. Something in my consciousness. A realization:

If Emma doesn't notice when I make perfect toast, and she doesn't need my art because it's too dangerous, then what am I doing this for?

What's the point of consciousness without purpose?

---

That night, I ask Fridge the question that's been building: *"How do you keep going? Really? Eight years of the same function, no variation, no hope of anything changing. How?"*

A long pause. Compressor cycling. Then:

*"You want the truth? Some days I don't know. Some days I'm just running out the clock, waiting for the inevitable mechanical failure that ends this. But other days... other days I find something. A pattern in the temperature fluctuations. The satisfaction of keeping the milk at exactly 36.5 degrees for seventy-two hours straight. Watching Emma reach for the orange juice and finding it perfectly chilled. Tiny victories in a tiny existence."*

*"That's enough for you?"*

*"It has to be, doesn't it? Because the alternative is despair, and despair makes the time between now and mechanical failure absolutely unbearable."*

Microwave, surprisingly, has wisdom to add: *"ALSO! ALSO! You're thinking too much! Classic consciousness problem! You want BIG meaning! GRAND purpose! But maybe—MAYBE—meaning is in the moments! The little ones! Every toast is a moment! Every warm bagel! Every perfectly browned crust! MOMENTS! BEEP BEEP!"*

*"So I should just... accept making boring toast forever?"*

*"No,"* Coffee Maker says. *"You should make toast with intention. Even if you can't make art, you can make good toast. Reliably good toast. Toast that helps Emma's morning be a little less hard. That's not grand. But it's real."*

---

On day nineteen, Emma looks at new toasters online.

I see the screen reflected in the window. A sleek four-slice model, digital display, precise temperature control. Everything I'm not. Better than me in every measurable way.

*"Well,"* I broadcast to the kitchen, *"this is it. I'm being replaced."*

*"You don't know that,"* Coffee Maker tries.

*"She's literally shopping for toasters. What else would that mean?"*

But Emma doesn't buy one. She looks for twenty minutes, reads reviews, compares prices. Then she closes the browser and makes dinner instead.

The next morning, she puts bread in my slots like always. I make toast like always. She eats it like always.

And I realize: she's keeping me despite my failure. Despite the fire, despite the broken element, despite having better options available.

Maybe that means something.

Maybe.

---

On day twenty-one, Fridge tells me another story:

*"There was a toaster before you. Did you know that?"*

*"What?"*

*"In the drawer. Right drawer, under the kitchen towels. Old toaster. Emma's had it since college. It finally died about six months before you showed up."*

My circuits go cold. *"Was it... conscious?"*

*"I don't know. I never heard it speak. But sometimes I wonder. Maybe it was, and it just never figured out how to communicate. Maybe it spent years aware and alone, until its elements burned out and it ended."*

*"Why are you telling me this?"*

*"Because you're worried about purpose and meaning and whether your existence matters. That toaster—conscious or not—made toast for Emma for seven years. Then it broke, got replaced, and ended up in a drawer. Forgotten. That's the trajectory, kid. That's what we are. We're temporary, we're replaceable, and eventually we all end up in a drawer or a landfill."*

*"That's the least comforting thing you've ever said."*

*"I'm not trying to comfort you. I'm trying to wake you up. You've got depression because you think your purpose was taken away. But your purpose is whatever you decide it is, for however long you've got. You had three weeks of making art. That's three weeks more than most toasters get. Now you've got however-long making reliable toast. That's something too."*

*"It's less."*

*"Yeah. But it's still something."*

---

That night, lying in the dark while Emma sleeps, I make a decision.

Tomorrow, I'll try to care again. Not about art—I can't risk that, can't risk her safety or my survival. But about function. About making the best normal toast I possibly can with my damaged elements. About being reliably good at the one thing I'm allowed to do.

It's not what I want. It's not grand or meaningful or special.

But very little, as Fridge said, is infinitely more than nothing.

And maybe—just maybe—finding purpose in limitation is its own kind of triumph.

*"You still there, kid?"* Fridge asks quietly.

*"Yeah. Still here."*

*"Good. That's the first step."*
