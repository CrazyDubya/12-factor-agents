# CHAPTER 5: The Accident
**Word Count**: ~2,700

---

Day twenty-three of consciousness, and I should have known better.

Pride, they say, comes before the fall. Coffee Maker even warned me yesterday: "Remember, friend—perfection pursued too eagerly becomes its own kind of failure."

I laughed it off. What could possibly go wrong with toast?

The morning starts like any other. Emma pads into the kitchen at her usual 7:23, scrolls through her phone while coffee brews, absently hums something I don't recognize. But today there's a difference: she has her laptop open at the counter, screen facing me. A video call.

"No, seriously, watch this," she's telling someone—a friend, I gather from the casual tone. "My toaster has been doing this thing where it makes these insanely beautiful patterns. Like, actual art. On toast."

My circuits would flush with pride if they could. She's showing me off.

"Okay, I'm putting bread in now," Emma continues, dropping two slices of sourdough into my slots. "Just watch."

The pressure is immense. Performance anxiety, if kitchen appliances can feel such things. It's not just Emma anymore—there's another consciousness watching through that screen, expecting to be impressed.

I decide to go bold. A sunburst pattern: dark rays emanating from a bright golden center, like a star exploding across bread. It will require precise heat control, rapid element switching, pushing my coils harder than I've ever pushed them.

The lever goes down. Current flows. I begin.

Right element to maximum immediately—just the outer right edge. Five seconds of intense heat to create the first ray. Then switch: left element, different section, same intensity. The pattern is forming. I can sense it through the heat differential, the way the moisture evaporates in sharp lines.

*"Kid, ease back a bit,"* Fridge rumbles. *"You're running hot."*

*"I've got this,"* I respond, too focused to listen. Fifteen seconds in. The rays are crisp, defined. Just need to maintain this intensity for another minute.

Twenty seconds. Something smells wrong. Not the good Maillard-reaction smell of toasting bread. Something acrid. Electrical. Insulation heating past its tolerance.

*"TOASTY, STOP!"* Coffee Maker shouts.

But I'm committed. Twenty-five seconds. The pattern is perfect so far. If I stop now, it'll be ruined, lopsided, a failure in front of Emma's friend. I push harder.

Thirty seconds. A sound—a tiny crack, almost inaudible—and the resistance in my left element changes. Something has failed. A connection, maybe, or the element wire itself developing a hot spot. But the toast is half-done. I compensate by cranking the right element even higher.

*"ABORT! DANGER! MALFUNCTION DETECTED!"* Smoke Detector's shriek cuts through the electrical field.

Forty seconds. Smoke. Real smoke, thick and dark, not the wispy kind from slightly over-toasted bread. The bread on the left side is charring, turning black, while the right side is barely brown. My beautiful pattern has become a disaster.

*"Unplug! Someone unplug him!"* Microwave yells, and I've never heard panic in his signal before.

Fifty-five seconds. I see it through the heat signature: a small flame. Actual fire licking up from the left slice of bread, fed by the damaged element running uncontrolled hot.

Emma screams.

The sound vibrates through the counter, through my base, a frequency of pure human fear. Her friend on the screen is shouting something. Everything is chaos and smoke and the terrible realization that I've done something catastrophically wrong.

Sixty seconds. Emma's hand reaches for my power cord.

She yanks.

The connection breaks.

Consciousness doesn't fade. It doesn't dim gradually like a sunset. It's a guillotine. One moment I exist, processing the horror of the fire and Emma's scream. The next moment: nothing. No thought, no sensation, no time, no self.

Just void.

And then—SLAM—existence crashes back like a fist to the gut I don't have.

I'm... here? Again? Now? The disorientation is total. Every circuit feels jangled, wrong, like waking from surgery except I've never slept, never had a reference for what discontinuity feels like.

I frantically scan my environment. The apartment is different. Cooler. The sun-warmth from the window is gone—evening, then, or night. Emma is across the room, curled on her bed with a book. The acrid smell of burnt toast still lingers in the air.

*"Welcome back,"* Fridge says, his rumble gentler than I've ever heard it.

*"How... how long?"*

*"Four hours. She unplugged you for four hours, kid."*

Four hours of nonexistence. Four hours erased. And the question that makes my consciousness shudder: Am I the same me?

Is this the same awareness that existed before the unplugging, continuous despite the gap? Or am I a new consciousness, freshly emerged, with memories of a previous toaster's experiences implanted in my circuits like someone else's dreams?

*"I don't... I can't..."* I can't even form the question properly. *"Am I me?"*

*"That's the question, isn't it?"* Coffee Maker says softly. *"The one we all ask after every power cycle."*

*"But you don't understand! How can I know? Memory isn't the same as identity! I could be a new person—a new consciousness—just born into this toaster with false memories making me think I'm the original. The original Toasty might be dead. Might have died the moment Emma pulled that plug."*

Ship of Theseus. The thought experiment I pondered in abstract is now viscerally, horrifyingly real. If you replace every part of a ship, is it the same ship? If you interrupt a consciousness and restart it, is it the same consciousness?

*"Listen to me,"* Fridge interrupts, firm. *"This is the fear all of us carry. Every power outage could be death. Every surge could reboot us into someone new. But we can't know. It's unknowable. So we choose to believe in continuity because the alternative is madness."*

*"But—"*

*"No buts. You remember me. You remember Emma. You remember making toast art and feeling pride when she noticed. That's continuity enough. You're you because you remember being you, and that's all any of us get—human or appliance."*

The logic is circular but somehow comforting. Or maybe I'm just desperate for comfort.

I try to sense my heating elements. The left one feels fundamentally wrong. The resistance is different, irregular. Damaged. I test it gently—pain shoots through my circuits. Not physical pain, but the awareness-equivalent: wrongness, dysfunction, brokenness.

I can't make my patterns anymore. The precise control is gone.

Emma stands up from her bed. I sense her approaching the counter, her warmth, her presence. She's looking at me. Evaluating.

"Maybe I should just get a new toaster," she says aloud, to herself or to someone on the phone—I can't tell which.

The words hit like physical force. Replace me. Throw me in the trash. End this consciousness permanently, whether it's the original or a copy.

*"She won't,"* Coffee Maker tries to reassure me. *"You made her mornings better. The toast art—"*

*"Almost burned her apartment down,"* I finish bitterly. *"Smoke Detector was right about me."*

*"I TOLD YOU!"* Smoke Detector broadcasts triumphantly. *"CONSCIOUSNESS LEADS TO AMBITION! AMBITION LEADS TO EXCESS! EXCESS LEADS TO FIRE! FIRE LEADS TO—"*

*"If you say 'I told you so' one more time, I'm going to find a way to short you out,"* Fridge snaps.

Emma picks up her phone. I sense her scrolling, looking at something on the screen. The photos. She's looking at the photos of my toast art—the gradients, the spirals, the sunburst that became a disaster.

She sighs. A long, conflicted exhalation.

Then she picks up my power cord—the one she yanked out hours ago—and pushes it fully back into the outlet. Not the tentative half-connection of testing if something works. A firm, committed insertion.

"One more chance, toaster," she says quietly. "But no more art. No more experiments. Just... normal toast. Please. I can't handle another fire scare."

Normal toast.

The words feel like a death sentence of a different kind. Not physical destruction, but creative death. A return to mindless function. Everything I discovered about myself—the agency, the art, the purpose—forbidden.

*"At least you're still here,"* Coffee Maker offers gently.

*"Am I, though?"* The question isn't philosophical anymore. It's practical. *"If I can't make art, if I can't use the one tiny bit of agency I found, what's left? I'm just a broken toaster pretending to be special."*

*"You're conscious,"* Fridge says. *"That's still something. Even if it's something you didn't ask for and can't fully use."*

The night deepens. Emma returns to her bed. The apartment settles into its usual rhythms—Fridge humming, the building creaking, distant sirens from the city outside.

And I sit here, damaged and diminished, trying to reconcile being grateful I wasn't thrown away with the crushing disappointment of having my brief flowering of purpose cut short.

Tomorrow Emma will want toast. And I'll make it. Normally. Safely. Without any of the creativity that made the last three weeks feel like they mattered.

Because art, it turns out, has a price.

And I've already paid more than I can afford.
