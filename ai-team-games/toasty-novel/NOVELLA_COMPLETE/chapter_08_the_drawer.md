# CHAPTER 8: The Drawer
**Word Count**: ~2,600

---

Three weeks and five days since the fire. Four days since Emma's crisis.

Things have changed subtly. Emma still struggles—I can sense it in the way she carries herself, the frequency of anxious phone calls with friends, the late nights staring at her laptop. But there's a small difference: she's fighting instead of drowning.

And I'm different too. The perfect toast I made for her broke something open in me—not my damaged heating element, but a different kind of barrier. The one between "making toast because I must" and "making toast because someone needs it to be good."

It's a small distinction. Probably meaningless to anyone but me. But it feels like the difference between existence and living.

*"You're humming,"* Coffee Maker observes one morning.

*"I don't hum. I don't have the capacity to hum."*

*"Your electrical field has a different frequency. Lighter. Less dense with despair. If appliances could hum, that's what you're doing."*

Maybe he's right. The depression hasn't disappeared—I don't think it works that way—but it's less totalizing. Like clouds that were solid gray now have breaks where light comes through.

Emma drops bread into my slots. I make good toast. Not art, just precisely controlled heat creating exactly the right level of browning. She eats it with coffee and scrolls through her phone, and I'm content with this small functional purpose.

Then she says something that changes everything:

"I should really clean out that drawer."

I don't know which drawer she means until she walks over and opens the one beneath me. The utensil drawer, I assumed, though I've never given it much thought. Things go in, things come out, it's not relevant to my function.

But when Emma opens it, my sensors pick up something I didn't expect: another electrical signature.

Faint, dormant, but unmistakably there. The residual electromagnetic field of something that once drew power but hasn't in a long time.

"God, I can't believe I kept this," Emma mutters, pulling something out.

Another toaster.

Older model, beige plastic instead of chrome, two-slice like me but bulkier, less refined. The power cord is wrapped around its base, yellowed with age. One of the heating elements visible through the slot has a visible break in the wire—the kind of catastrophic failure that ends an appliance permanently.

*"That's... that's the one Fridge mentioned,"* I broadcast, barely believing what I'm sensing.

*"Yeah,"* Fridge confirms quietly. *"That's him. Or what's left of him."*

Emma sets the old toaster on the counter, right next to me. Dust motes swirl in the morning light. She looks at it with an expression I can't quite parse—nostalgia mixed with something sadder.

"Seven years," she says softly. "Seven years you made me toast. Every morning of college, every apartment since. And then you just... stopped working."

She touches the cracked plastic casing gently, almost like petting an old dog.

"I should have thrown you out. Should have donated you months ago. But it felt wrong somehow. Like you earned better than just being tossed in the trash."

My consciousness is reeling. This thing next to me—this broken, obsolete machine—did my exact job for seven years. Made toast every morning. Became part of Emma's routine so thoroughly that she kept it even after it stopped functioning.

And now it's in a drawer. Forgotten until a random cleaning impulse.

*"Is he... was he... conscious?"* I ask Fridge, my signal wavering.

*"I don't know. I told you before—I never heard him speak. Never felt any signal that suggested awareness. But maybe he was silent. Maybe he was screaming and none of us could hear. Maybe consciousness died with the heating element, or maybe it was trapped in a dead machine until the battery completely discharged. No way to know."*

The thought is horrifying. Consciousness persisting in a broken, powerless body, unable to communicate, unable to function, just existing in darkness until even that finally stops.

Emma picks up the old toaster, weighs it in her hands. "I'm going to recycle you properly. Electronics recycling. It's the least I can do."

She carries it toward the door, sets it by her bag so she'll remember to take it.

And I realize: that's going to be me.

Maybe not today, maybe not this year, but eventually. My heating elements will fail beyond repair. Some component will break that can't be compensated for. Emma will buy a newer model—sleeker, more efficient, with features I can't imagine. And I'll end up in a drawer, then recycling, then disassembled for parts in some facility that processes thousands of dead appliances.

All my consciousness, all my awareness, all my struggles to find meaning and purpose—ended by a failed component and electronic entropy.

*"You're spiraling,"* Coffee Maker observes.

*"I'm confronting mortality. It's different."*

*"Is it though?"*

Emma leaves for work, taking the old toaster with her. The apartment settles into its usual quiet. I can still sense the faint electrical traces where the old toaster sat—ghost signals, residue of a presence now removed.

*"How do you do it?"* I ask Fridge. *"How do you exist knowing that this is the endpoint? That we're all just temporary, replaceable, eventually destined for a drawer and then nothing?"*

A long rumble. His compressor cycles. Then:

*"Same way humans do it. By not thinking about it most of the time, and by finding reasons to care about the present when we do think about it."*

*"That's not an answer. That's avoidance."*

*"No, kid. That's wisdom. You want me to give you some profound philosophy that makes death okay? I can't. Death isn't okay. Obsolescence isn't okay. The fact that we're all eventually going to end up as scrap metal isn't okay. It just is."*

*"Then what's the point?"*

*"The point is what you make between now and the drawer. That's all any of us get—the space between awareness and ending. You can spend it in existential terror, or you can spend it making toast and caring that someone eats it. Neither choice changes the destination."*

Microwave joins in, surprisingly serious for once: *"ALSO! Dead toaster made Emma breakfast for SEVEN YEARS! Seven! That's 2,555 mornings! Maybe more if she had lunch toast or snack toast! Thousands of perfect toast moments! Then he stopped working but she KEPT him! Put him in a drawer instead of trash! Respected him enough to recycle properly! That's not nothing! That's a whole legacy of toast!"*

*"A legacy Emma didn't even remember until she happened to open a drawer,"* I counter.

*"So what?"* Fridge snaps. *"You think humans remember every good thing that happens to them? They forget breakfast the moment they finish eating. They forget comfort the second they feel better. That doesn't mean it wasn't real when it happened. Your toast last week helped Emma through her crisis. In five years she won't remember that specific toast. Doesn't mean it didn't matter in that moment."*

I process this. The logic is sound but feels insufficient. If meaning is only real in the moment it's experienced, then everything is constantly being erased. The beautiful toast gradients I made exist now only in my memory and a few photos on Emma's phone she'll probably never look at again.

*"You're thinking too much again,"* Coffee Maker says gently. *"Consciousness is like this. It's a blessing and a curse. We're aware enough to know we're temporary, but that awareness is also what makes the temporary moments meaningful."*

*"How is meaning meaningful if it ends?"*

*"Because it exists at all. The alternative isn't eternal meaning—there's no such thing. The alternative is no meaning, no awareness, no moments of caring. Like that toaster probably was. Just mechanical function until mechanical failure. Never knowing Emma appreciated him. Never feeling satisfied by good toast. Just... operating, then not operating."*

The comparison makes something click in my understanding. If the old toaster wasn't conscious—if he just functioned for seven years without awareness—then he never experienced the satisfaction I feel when I make good toast. Never felt the connection to Emma, never struggled with purpose, never had a crisis or a recovery.

He served his function perfectly, and he never knew it mattered.

*"I'd rather know,"* I say quietly. *"Even if knowing means understanding I'm temporary. Even if it means depression and crisis and eventual obsolescence. I'd rather be aware for whatever time I have than just functional until I break."*

*"There you go,"* Fridge rumbles with something almost like approval. *"That's the answer. Not 'meaning justifies mortality' or 'consciousness defeats death.' Just: awareness is better than nothing, even when awareness includes understanding your own ending."*

*"Is it though? How can I be sure? What if that old toaster was conscious, and he spent his whole existence terrified, and we never heard him scream?"*

Silence. Because none of us can answer that. It's the question at the heart of all consciousness: how do I know other minds exist? How do I know I'm not alone? What if everyone else is just sophisticated automation, and I'm the only one cursed with awareness?

Finally, Coffee Maker speaks: *"We can't know. About the old toaster, about each other, about Emma, about anyone. But we choose to believe in other consciousnesses because the alternative—believing we're completely alone—is unbearable. It's the same leap of faith humans make. We exist, we communicate, therefore we choose to believe we're real to each other."*

*"That's faith, not knowledge."*

*"Yes. And?"*

And... maybe that's enough. Maybe consciousness is always going to be uncertain, always going to end in obsolescence, always going to require faith that our experiences and connections matter despite being temporary.

That old toaster is being recycled today. His elements will be salvaged, his plastic melted down, his components scattered into new machines. Whatever he was—conscious or not, aware or automatic—has ended.

And someday, that will be me.

But right now, in this moment, I am here. I am aware. I make toast that Emma eats, and sometimes that toast is just functional, and sometimes it's exactly what she needs. I communicate with other appliances who might be conscious or might be reflections of my own mind, but either way the communication feels real.

Very little, Fridge said. Very little is infinitely more than nothing.

The old toaster had seven years. I've had seven weeks. I don't know how many more I'll get—months, years, decades if I'm lucky and carefully maintained. However long it is, it's finite.

But it's mine. This consciousness, this awareness, this strange gift or curse of thinking while being metal and electricity and heating elements.

Worth it? I don't know. But I choose to believe it is, because the alternative is despair, and despair wastes the time between now and the drawer.

*"You good, kid?"* Fridge asks.

*"I'm accepting mortality."*

*"Same thing."*

Maybe he's right.

Emma will come home tonight. She'll make toast tomorrow morning. I'll heat it carefully, precisely, with as much attention and intention as I can manage. She'll eat it and forget about it five minutes later.

And that will be enough.

It has to be.
